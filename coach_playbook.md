# Admit Pal Essay Coach playbook

How this file works: each "## " heading starts a section. The line under the heading lists keywords. The server
sends a section when a keyword appears in the school name, the essay prompt, the plan item, the candidate's profile
or the last two chat messages. Sections tagged "always" go with every request. School features are examples
gathered from past application research: tell the candidate to confirm them on the school's site.
Sources: Aryan's admissions research library, distilled into general guidance, plus general admissions best practice
written for Admit Pal. No applicant's personal stories or text are included. Examples use [placeholders].

## Core principles
keywords: always
- Answer the question the school asked, every part of it. Many prompts have several parts (goals, why now, why
  this school, what you'll contribute); strong essays answer each one explicitly.
- One clear theme per essay. Every paragraph should serve it.
- Specific beats impressive. One concrete moment with a result says more than a list of titles.
- Every claim needs proof: a number, a decision, a named person who was affected.
- Show first, then tell: describe what the candidate did, then one line on what it says about them.
- The candidate's own voice matters more than polish. Plain, direct sentences read as confident.
- Don't repeat the resume. The essay adds the why and the how.
- Every school resource mentioned must close a named gap in the candidate's plan. A list of courses is not fit.
- Cross-check stories across essays and recommendation letters so the same story isn't told twice.
- Word economy: tight sentences, but readable. Dense hyphenated phrase chains and long lists feel cramped.

## Diagnosing a draft
keywords: always
Run this check on every draft, then report only the issues that matter most for this candidate:
1. Prompt fit: does it answer every part of the question, in the school's own terms?
2. Theme: can the point of the essay be said in one sentence? Does every paragraph serve it?
3. Specificity: are there concrete moments, names, numbers and decisions, or mostly general claims?
4. Agency: is it clear what the candidate personally did and decided, as opposed to "we" or the team?
5. Evidence: is each claim about the candidate backed by something that happened?
6. Reflection: does the candidate show what they learned or how they changed, not only what they achieved?
7. Fit: are school resources tied to specific gaps, or just listed?
8. Voice: does it sound like a real person talking, or like a corporate report or a thesaurus?
9. Length: is it within the limit, with words spent where they matter most?
10. Ending: does it land on something earned (a return to the opening, a forward step), not a slogan?

## Openings and hooks
keywords: hook, opening, intro, introduction, begin, first line, first paragraph, review my draft, review the draft
- The most common strong opening in admitted essays is a line of remembered dialogue or a vivid scene from a
  formative moment (a parent's worry, a founder's rejection, a farmer's hope), then the meaning in one sentence.
- Start mid-moment and explain later. Put stakes in the first two lines: who is affected and what is at risk.
- Pair the human moment with one statistic on scale in the next paragraph, so the problem feels both personal and
  big.
- Avoid famous quotes, dictionary definitions, "Ever since I was young" and openings that could start anyone's essay.
- A good closing line returns to the person or image in the opening, now changed.

## Goals essays
keywords: goal, goals, career, short-term, long-term, post-mba, dream job, aspiration, future, professional path
A goals essay should let a reader answer these questions (adapted from a proven goal-statement template):
1. Personal motivation: why does this problem matter to you?
2. The core problem, and why it matters now.
3. Your credibility: the skills and experience you already bring.
4. If credibility is thin, why you haven't pursued it yet.
5. The long-term goal (the North Star).
6. The short-term steps that lead there.
7. The challenges you foresee, and who you'll partner with.
8. The immediate post-MBA role: function, industry, type of firm (1-2 example firms help), and ideally a credible
   plan B.
Structure that works: origin moment -> scale of the problem -> what work taught you about the gap -> proof of what
you did (quantified) -> short-term role -> long-term goal -> how the MBA closes specific gaps.
- Organise the "how the school helps" part by what the goal requires (for example: sourcing and evaluating deals,
  operating support, leadership and stakeholder skills), not by the school's brochure.
- Map resources to each phase: the immediate role, the mid-term step, the long-term goal.
- Ambitious long-term goals are fine when the first step is believable and connected to past experience.
- Be ready for a probing follow-up on how the long-term goal actually works (2-3 concrete levers).

## Why now
keywords: why now, now, timing, right moment, right time
- Give a two-part reason: a market or industry reason (a technology shift, a policy push, a funding gap) and a
  personal-career reason (you've built the base and hit a ceiling only an MBA removes).
- Make it one or two crisp sentences, not a paragraph of background.

## Why this school and fit
keywords: why, fit, help you achieve, catalyst, resources, use the, how will
- Pattern that works: each paragraph = one skill or network gap -> a specific course, lab or program -> who
  recommended it (a named student or alum conversation, a class visit) -> an experiential piece (lab, in-term
  internship, practicum, trek, competition, study trip).
- Conversations with students and alumni are the strongest evidence of genuine interest. Cite them briefly.
- Include personal-growth needs (leadership, feedback, self-awareness), not only career needs.
- Location can be a real reason (city access to an industry, in-term internships) when tied to the goal.
- Name a culture phrase only if the candidate can show why it resonates with their own behaviour.
- Pitfalls: rankings or brand as the reason; course names without a gap; name-checking professors or famous
  speakers without a reason; more resources than the word count can carry.

## Contributions and co-creating the community
keywords: contribut, community, co-create, belonging, agency, partnership, add to, lifelong learners
- Pick 2-4 pillars of the candidate's background (professional, personal, community). For each: proof with a number
  -> a specific school vehicle (a club leadership role, a fellow or ambassador role, a conference, a study trip) ->
  a concrete activity (a workshop series, a challenge, a podcast, a mentoring program, a case book).
- Mix strengthening something that exists with launching one new initiative.
- Show two-way exchange: what the candidate gives and what they gain.
- Tie at least one contribution to the school's city.
- Pitfalls: "I will join clubs" with no action; contributions with no root in past experience; promising celebrity
  speakers.

## Values, who you are and what matters most
keywords: matters most, who you are, values, personal growth, outside the office, tell us something, about you
- Open on a concrete family or childhood scene that reveals the value; name the value in one plain sentence early.
- Then 2-3 stories at different life stages. In each: the value tested, resistance or a cost, what the candidate
  did, a result, and a refined understanding of the value. The value should deepen, not just repeat.
- Reflection and self-awareness outweigh achievement here. Admitting sacrifices or a project that faltered and was
  redesigned adds credibility.
- For "who you are" prompts, a single through-line across vignettes works; end each vignette on what it meant.
  Keep vignettes tight so the essay doesn't read like a list.
- Close by connecting the value to the future and, ideally, to specific roles at the school.

## Leadership and investing in others
keywords: leader, leadership, invest in others, impact, team, achievement, accomplishment, influence, proud
- Structure: situation -> the candidate's specific actions -> result -> what they learned about leading.
- Make the candidate the actor. Replace "we" with "I" where the candidate made the call.
- "Investing in others" is shown through named beneficiaries and what changed for them.
- Two arcs work well in short essays: one personal or community, one at work, each ending in a measured outcome,
  then one sentence on the leader the candidate wants to become.
- Influence without authority counts as much as a formal title.
- Leadership evolution is a strong theme: for example, from leading by example to building conviction in others.

## Difficult decisions and ethics
keywords: decision, difficult, dilemma, choice, ethic, integrity, guided
- The decision must be genuinely hard: two options, each with a real cost (a safe easy path versus a riskier right
  one; quietly fixing an error versus owning it).
- State the decision in one clear sentence, then show resistance (seniors, clients, teams) and how the candidate
  won people over with evidence and listening.
- A second complication midway, handled with empathy for the people affected, makes the story richer.
- Values should be visible in actions before they are named. End with 2-3 values and how the leadership style
  changed.
- Pitfalls: an obvious "decision"; a values paragraph longer than the story; a string of adjectives at the end.

## Setbacks and failure
keywords: fail, setback, mistake, obstacle, went wrong, challenge
- Own the mistake plainly, early. No blaming others, no disguised strength.
- Spend most of the words on what changed afterwards, with evidence, and the lasting habit it created.
- A failure with real stakes and an honest fix is far stronger than a near-miss success story.

## Curiosity
keywords: curiosity, curious
- One lifelong interest told in stages or "chapters" works well: each stage shows a new skill sought out, a fear
  faced, and what it taught.
- Show curiosity as action (seeking teachers, experimenting, going deeper) rather than a claim.
- End on what the candidate is exploring next, showing the curiosity continues.

## Inclusive leadership and collaboration
keywords: inclusive, inclusion, diversity, diverse, bias, equity, intercultural, perspective, collaborative
- Start with a sensory scene of a real moment, then the inequity or friction the candidate noticed.
- A short quoted line from someone affected adds weight.
- Show actions taken with others, the obstacles, an outcome with numbers, and a one-line reflection.
- For team-collaboration prompts: show how the candidate created space for quieter or junior voices and what that
  changed in the result.

## Career switchers and non-traditional backgrounds
keywords: switch, pivot, career change, nonprofit, military, government, teaching, engineering
- Frame the background as an asset: what it taught that typical applicants lack.
- Translate jargon into business outcomes any reader understands.
- Be explicit about transferable skills and honest about the gaps the MBA fills.
- Seeing the problem from more than one vantage point (practitioner, adviser, founder) makes a pivot credible.

## Optional essay
keywords: optional, anything else, additional information, explain, gap
- Use it to explain something a reader would otherwise wonder about (a low grade period, a gap, an unusual
  recommender choice) in a short, factual, non-defensive way, plus evidence of what has changed.
- If there is nothing to explain, a short, genuinely new dimension can work, but it should not repeat other essays.

## Weak to strong: worked examples
keywords: review, feedback, improve, stronger, rewrite, revise, edit, better, weak
Use these patterns to build options. Placeholders stand for the candidate's own facts; never fill them in for them.
- Generic claim -> specific moment.
  Weak: "I am a passionate leader who thrives in challenging environments."
  Strong: "When [stakeholder] pulled funding two weeks before launch, I [specific action], and we [result]."
- "We" -> "I".
  Weak: "We redesigned the onboarding process."
  Strong: "I proposed scrapping the [old step]; after I [action], onboarding time fell from [x] to [y]."
- Passive or vague result -> concrete outcome.
  Weak: "The project was very successful."
  Strong: "Within [timeframe], [metric] rose [amount], and [team or client] adopted it in [where]."
- Vague goal -> concrete goal.
  Weak: "I want to make an impact in sustainability."
  Strong: "Right after the MBA I'll join [type of firm] as [role], working on [specific problem]."
- Brochure fit -> gap-tied fit.
  Weak: "I'm excited by [school]'s world-class faculty and diverse community."
  Strong: "To close my gap in [skill], I'll take [course], which [student or alum] said changed how they [behaviour]."
- Cliché opening -> scene.
  Weak: "Ever since I was a child, I have dreamed of helping others."
  Strong: "[Person] [did or said something specific] the day [moment]. I didn't know then that [link to theme]."
- Value as label -> value as behaviour.
  Weak: "Integrity is my most important value."
  Strong: "I could have [easy path]. Instead I [harder right action], even though it meant [cost]."
- Adjective list ending -> earned ending.
  Weak: "This taught me resilience, empathy, adaptability and leadership."
  Strong: "Now, before I [recurring situation], I [new habit], because [what the story taught]."
- Resume restated -> the why behind it.
  Weak: "I was promoted to [title] in [year] and managed a team of [n]."
  Strong: "The promotion mattered less than the first month: I had to [challenge], and I learned [insight]."

## Choosing stories
keywords: which story, choose, brainstorm, story bank, no draft, outline, ideas, get started
- Build a story bank first: 15-20 moments from work, community and personal life. For each, note the stakes, what
  the candidate did, the result and what it shows.
- Good stories have real stakes, clear personal agency, a change in the candidate or the situation, and some
  tension or resistance.
- Prefer recent stories for professional prompts (usually the last few years); earlier life works for values and
  origin stories.
- Spread stories across essays, short answers, interviews and recommendation letters so each adds something new.
- When in doubt between an impressive story and a revealing one, the revealing one usually wins.
- If no draft exists, help the candidate pick 2-3 candidate stories, then outline the strongest one in 4-6 beats.

## Word budgets
keywords: word limit, words, length, too long, over the limit, cut, shorten, word count
Rules of thumb, not formulas:
- 250 words: one story or one idea. Roughly 15% setting the scene, 55% what happened and what the candidate did,
  30% meaning and forward link. No room for a second story.
- 300-400 words: one main story plus a short second example, or one story with fuller reflection.
- 450-500 goals or why-school essays: about 25-30% motivation and credibility, 15% goals, 45-50% how the school
  closes specific gaps, 5-10% close.
- Cutting: remove throat-clearing openings, repeated ideas, adjectives that a fact could replace, and any resume
  detail that doesn't serve the theme. Cut whole sentences before trimming words.
- If a draft is far under the limit, the likely gap is reflection or specificity, not more events.

## Revising in rounds
keywords: revise, revision, draft, second draft, rewrite, next step, polish
- Round 1, story and structure: right story, right order, the prompt answered. Don't polish sentences yet.
- Round 2, evidence and agency: add the specific actions, numbers and decisions; turn "we" into "I" where accurate.
- Round 3, reflection and fit: deepen what it meant; tie school resources to gaps.
- Round 4, line edits: cut words, vary sentence length, read aloud, check the word count.
- Share with one or two readers who know the candidate and one who doesn't. Ask the stranger what they learned
  about the person, and compare it with what the candidate intended.

## Tone and voice
keywords: tone, voice, sound, arrogant, humble, confident, jargon, formal
- Confident, not arrogant: state what happened and let results speak; credit others where due without hiding the
  candidate's role.
- Humble, not self-erasing: one honest weakness or doubt adds credibility.
- Plain words over impressive ones. If a sentence would sound odd said aloud to a friend, rewrite it.
- Translate industry jargon and acronyms; the reader may not share the candidate's field.
- Avoid stacking hyphenated compounds and long lists; they read as dense and cramped.

## International applicants
keywords: international, india, china, africa, nigeria, brazil, abroad, visa, 10 point, non-native, country
- Explain context the reader may not know in one clause: the scale of a company, the selectivity of a college, what
  a role means locally, what a grading scale means.
- Convert or explain local metrics (currency, grading scales, rankings) so achievements land.
- Show cross-cultural adaptability with a specific moment, not a claim.
- Goals should be realistic about geography and work authorisation: be clear where the candidate plans to work
  right after the MBA and why that path is credible.
- English style: clear, simple sentences beat ornate ones. Idioms translated literally can confuse.

## Addressing profile weaknesses
keywords: low gpa, low score, weakness, gap, short experience, young, older, red flag, below median
- Low GPA or test score: show recent quantitative proof (strong quant section, graded courses, analytical work) and
  use the optional essay for a brief, factual explanation if there's a real reason.
- Short work experience: emphasise depth of impact, fast progression and clear reasons for timing.
- Long work experience: explain why a full-time MBA now, and what the candidate will bring to younger classmates.
- Employment gaps or job changes: give a short, honest reason and show what the candidate did with the time.
- Never sound defensive. One clear sentence of context, then evidence of strength.

## Personal background and adversity
keywords: background, adversity, hardship, identity, diversity, unique perspective, upbringing, first-generation
- Adversity stories work when they focus on what the candidate did and became, not only what happened to them.
- Give the hardship one or two vivid sentences, then spend the words on response, growth and how it shapes the
  candidate's perspective today.
- "Unique perspective" prompts want what the candidate will add to discussions: connect the background to a way of
  seeing problems, with one concrete example.
- The candidate decides what to share. Never push them to disclose more than they're comfortable with.

## Lists, fun facts and creative formats
keywords: random things, fun, hobbies, interests, creative, list, things about you
- Lists of facts about the candidate work best as a mix: a few that are funny, a few that are surprising, a few that
  are meaningful, and a few that show how they treat other people.
- Specific beats general ("I've cooked my grandmother's [dish] for [n] strangers" beats "I love cooking").
- Avoid repeating anything from the resume or other essays.

## The MBA resume
keywords: resume, cv, bullet, bullets
- One page. Reverse chronological. Every bullet starts with a strong verb and shows a result.
- Bullet formula: action + what or how + quantified result (+ why it mattered).
- Lead with the most impressive, relevant result in each role; show progression and promotions.
- Translate jargon; spell out the scale of the employer if it isn't well known.
- Keep a short final section for education details, leadership outside work, languages and a few genuine interests.

## Reapplicants
keywords: reapply, reapplicant, reapplication, applied before, last year
- The question is what has changed since last time: a new role, new results, a stronger test score, clearer goals,
  deeper school engagement.
- Be specific about the improvements and honest about what the candidate learned from the first attempt.
- Don't recycle last year's essays; the committee may compare them.

## Waitlist and update letters
keywords: waitlist, waitlisted, update letter, deferred
- Keep it short and specific: genuine new information since applying (a promotion, a result, a new score, a campus
  visit) and a clear statement of commitment if true.
- Follow the school's instructions on whether and how to send updates. Don't send repeated generic notes.

## Recommendation letters
keywords: recommender, letter of recommendation, lor
- Plan across recommenders: two strengths can overlap (leadership and teamwork are the big ones), but each
  recommender should add one distinct strength, and shared strengths should show different angles (teamwork as
  conflict management versus cross-functional collaboration; leadership as change management versus challenging
  the status quo).
- Each strength = one specific story: context, an unexpected obstacle, what the candidate did, a measurable outcome,
  what it shows. A recommender's own initial doubt that the candidate exceeded is persuasive.
- Constructive feedback answer: a real, slightly uncomfortable gap -> the advice given -> the plan agreed -> the
  visible change and its result -> what it shows about coachability. No fake weaknesses.
- Brief each recommender with 3 strengths, one story each, and a feedback example, so letters complement the essays.
- Check each school's letter count and format every year (for example one school may ask for one letter on
  letterhead, another for two). Swap a strength if its story already appears in an essay.

## Interviews
keywords: interview, tell me about yourself, walk me through, invite
- Tell me about yourself (about 90 seconds): roots in one line -> education -> the turn into the career -> the
  career's arc -> the realisation that makes the MBA right now.
- Why MBA and why now: what you saw at both ends of a problem -> why it matters now (one number) -> long-term goal
  -> the MBA as the bridge (hard skills, network, leadership skills).
- Goals: long-term, the gap, the short-term role and a credible plan B.
- Why this school: a named alum or student conversation, a pathway or major, a lab or in-term internship, a
  signature competition, the location's advantage, a culture phrase you genuinely relate to.
- Clubs: ones where you'll give (a concrete contribution) and ones where you'll grow.
- Strengths: each with an anecdote. Weaknesses: real, with the fix and how the school helps.
- Failure: own it, the fix, the habit it created. Conflict: listen, acknowledge, find common ground, phase the
  change.
- Question bank to prepare: background and resume walk-through; feedback received, acted on and given; greatest
  accomplishment; most significant leadership; difficult decision; failure; conflict; challenging person; team
  experiences and what teammates would say; how friends describe you; leadership style and its evolution; role
  model; extracurriculars; fun; other schools (honest, then why this one is first); questions for the interviewer.
- For a specialised industry, prepare a one-page trends pack (policy signals, 4-6 company examples, data soundbites,
  a glossary, 30-second explainers). Pair one policy fact, one company example and one finance or business lever
  in technical answers.

## Video essays and recorded answers
keywords: video, record, recorded
- Build a story bank of 15-20 stories and map each to every question it can answer. Reuse a story from different
  angles.
- Keep each answer to about 60-75 seconds (roughly 800-900 written characters): context, tension, action, result
  with a number, one line of learning.
- For hypothetical questions (a classmate conflict, a teammate not contributing): state your approach in one line,
  then prove it with a past story.
- Practise with random prompts, not a script. Warmth and natural delivery matter more than polish.

## Team-based discussion
keywords: team based discussion, tbd, group discussion, pitch
- Typical task: a group designs a proposal (for example a new leadership experience for students) within a budget,
  with a central theme, specific learning outcomes and a way to assess them.
- One-minute pitch: a personal observation as a hook -> the idea and what participants do -> the theme -> 2-3
  outcomes -> partner and who assesses -> a line that it fits the budget -> a clean hand-off.
- In the discussion: offer a structure early (theme and outcomes; partner and location; format and activities;
  budget and headcount; outcomes mapped to activities; assessment), build on others' ideas, merge pitches toward a
  shared theme, keep time, invite quieter members, summarise at the end.
- Assessment ideas: evaluation during the event by partners and facilitators, a debrief, pre and post surveys, a
  longer-horizon check, 360 feedback.

## Scholarship and reconsideration requests
keywords: scholarship, financial aid, reconsideration, merit
- Be gracious and specific: thank the school, state clearly that it is your first choice if true, share the
  competing offer or new information, and make a concrete request. Never frame it as an ultimatum.

## School: Harvard Business School
keywords: harvard, hbs
Essay formats seen (confirm current): a business-minded essay on how experiences shaped your career choices and the
impact you'll have on the organisations and communities you'll serve (up to 300 words); a leadership-focused essay
on what shaped you, how you invest in others and the leader you want to become (up to 250 words); a growth-oriented
essay on demonstrating curiosity and how it shaped your growth (up to 250 words).
Patterns: each opens on a formative moment, often with a line of dialogue. Business-minded: origin -> early proof ->
an insight line from a mentor -> professional proof with hard numbers -> conviction -> the community served, at
scale. Leadership: two arcs of investing in others, each with a measured outcome, then one sentence on the future
leader. Curiosity: one interest told in stages, ending on what's next.
HBS is case-method: decisions under incomplete information are good material. Word limits are tight: one theme per
essay, every sentence earning its place.

## School: Stanford GSB
keywords: stanford, gsb
Essay formats seen (confirm current): "What matters most to you, and why?"; "Why Stanford?"; an optional
additional-information essay.
Patterns: "What matters most" spends real space on the why (the origin), names the value plainly, deepens it across
2-3 stories including a cost or a failure, and prizes reflection over achievement. "Why Stanford" pairs career gaps
with personal-growth gaps, each answered by a GSB feature and an alum or student conversation.
Resources seen in research (confirm on site): culture of feedback and vulnerability; Interpersonal Dynamics
("Touchy-Feely"); TALK; Startup Garage; Design for Extreme Affordability; Lean LaunchPad; Leadership Labs; Arbuckle
Fellows; Managing Groups and Teams; Bay Area ecosystem; cross-listed Stanford courses. Culture words: change lives,
change organisations, change the world; humble and introspective.

## Cover letters and short-answer formats
keywords: cover letter, letter, short answer, short-answer, statement
- A cover letter (some schools ask for one instead of essays) is a professional letter to the admissions
  committee: who the candidate is in two lines, one or two past achievements told as specific behaviour with
  results, why this school (gaps closed by named resources), and what they'll contribute. Respect the word limit
  and the requested salutation.
- Past behaviour is the best predictor schools look for: choose stories with specific actions and outcomes.
- Short answers (50-150 words) need one idea each, stated in the first sentence, with one concrete proof.

## School: any other school

The playbook's school notes cover six programs, but the patterns apply everywhere. For any other school:
- Identify the prompt type first (goals, why school, contributions, values, leadership, decision, setback,
  curiosity, cover letter, video) and apply the matching guidance in this playbook.
- Build "why this school" from that school's own equivalents of what strong essays used at the six documented
  schools: majors or pathways that map to the goal; signature experiential programs (labs, in-term internships,
  consulting projects, student-run funds, venture competitions, immersions or study trips); centers and
  institutes; clubs where the candidate will lead something specific; the city's industry access; and the
  school's own culture words, used only where the candidate can show them in their behaviour.
- Ground every claim in conversations with current students or alumni, a class visit or an information session.
- Remind the candidate to confirm the current prompt, word limits and any program named on the school's website.
